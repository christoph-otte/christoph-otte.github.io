# Writing a Scientific Article

Scientific writing is not about sounding sophisticated — it is about being understood. A good article guides the reader from a clear question to a well-supported answer.

---

## 1. Structure of a Scientific Article (IMRaD)

Most natural science articles follow the **IMRaD** structure:

| Section | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Precise, informative, not a question |
| **Abstract** | Self-contained summary (~150–250 words) |
| **Introduction** | Motivation, background, research question |
| **Methods** | Reproducibility — what you did and how |
| **Results** | What you found — data, figures, key observations |
| **Discussion** | What it means, limitations, open questions |
| **Conclusion** | Brief summary of the take-home message |
| **References** | All cited sources, consistent format |

> Review articles and theoretical articles may deviate from this structure — that is fine. The key is that the structure must serve the reader.

---

## 2. Title and Abstract

**Title:**
- Should describe the content without being a full sentence ending in a question mark.
- Avoid jargon where possible; include key terms for searchability.
- Bad: *"Some Observations on Bird Migration"*
- Better: *"Latitudinal Variation in Departure Timing of European Migratory Songbirds"*

**Abstract:**
- Write it last, once the article is finished.
- It must stand alone — no citations, no undefined abbreviations, no figures.
- Include: motivation, method (briefly), main result, significance.
- Think of it as the article in miniature.

---

## 3. Introduction

The introduction answers: **Why should the reader care, and what exactly are you investigating?**

A good introduction moves from broad to specific:

1. **Context** — What is the field? What is the general problem or phenomenon?
2. **Gap** — What is not yet understood? What motivated your work?
3. **Approach** — What did you do? (Do not reveal results here.)
4. **Roadmap** (optional) — *"In Section 2, we describe… In Section 3, we present…"*

Keep the introduction focused. You do not need to survey the entire literature — cite the most relevant and most recent work.

---

## 4. Methods

The methods section must be **reproducible**. Someone with similar equipment and expertise should be able to repeat your work.

- Describe materials, instruments, and software with enough detail (model numbers, versions, settings).
- For computational work: describe algorithms, parameters, languages/libraries, and data sources.
- For experiments: include sample preparation, measurement protocol, calibration, and error handling.
- Refer to established methods by citation rather than restating them fully.
- Use past tense (*"we measured…"*, *"the simulation was run…"*).

---

## 5. Results

Present your findings **without interpretation** — save that for the Discussion.

- Lead with your main finding, then support it with data.
- Every figure and table must be referenced in the text and have a self-contained caption.
- Report numerical results with appropriate significant figures and uncertainties.
- Do not cherry-pick: include results that complicate your conclusion, and address them in the Discussion.

**Figure captions** should explain what is shown, what the axes represent, and what the key take-away is — without requiring the reader to look at the main text.

---

## 6. Discussion

The Discussion is where you **interpret** your results and connect them back to the field.

- Start by restating your main finding in plain language.
- Explain *why* you think you observed what you observed.
- Compare with prior work: agreement? Disagreement? Why?
- Acknowledge limitations honestly — reviewers will find them anyway.
- Suggest concrete next steps or open questions.

Do not introduce new results in the Discussion.

---

## 7. Conclusion

Keep it brief (one paragraph). Summarize:
1. What was the question?
2. What did you find?
3. What does it imply?

Avoid vague statements like *"This work has broad implications for science."* Be specific.

---

## 8. References

- Cite everything that is not common knowledge or your own original contribution.
- Use a consistent citation style throughout (we use **[specify: APA / Vancouver / ACS / custom]**).
- Prefer primary literature over textbooks or Wikipedia.
- Do not cite sources you have not read.
- All references must be reachable — ideally via DOI.

---

## 9. Scientific Writing Style

### Be precise, not impressive

Avoid inflated language. *"We used X"* is better than *"We employed the utilization of X."*

### Use the passive voice sparingly

The passive is conventional in methods sections (*"samples were prepared…"*) but the active voice is clearer everywhere else: *"We found that…"* rather than *"It was found that…"*

### One idea per sentence

Long sentences with multiple clauses are easy to misread. If a sentence contains more than ~25 words, consider splitting it.

### Define everything

Introduce all abbreviations at first use: *"magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)"*. Do not assume the reader shares your background.

### Hedge appropriately

Scientific language acknowledges uncertainty: *"suggest"*, *"indicate"*, *"appear to"*, *"under the conditions tested"*. Avoid absolute claims unless the evidence is overwhelming.

### Consistent terminology

Use the same word for the same thing throughout the article. Do not alternate between *"concentration"* and *"amount"* if you mean concentration.

---

## 10. Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| Burying the main result | State it early and clearly |
| Underdescribed methods | Add enough detail for reproducibility |
| Confusing results and discussion | Results = what; Discussion = why |
| Overreaching conclusions | Match claims to evidence |
| Missing uncertainty estimates | Always report errors / confidence intervals |
| Orphaned figures | Every figure must be cited in the text |
| First draft = final draft | Revise, then revise again |

---

## Checklist Before Submission

- [ ] Title is precise and informative
- [ ] Abstract is self-contained (no citations, no undefined abbreviations)
- [ ] Introduction has a clearly stated research question
- [ ] Methods are detailed enough to be reproduced
- [ ] All figures and tables are referenced and have captions
- [ ] Results and Discussion are clearly separated
- [ ] Limitations are acknowledged
- [ ] All claims are supported by data or citations
- [ ] References are complete and consistently formatted
- [ ] Spelling and grammar checked (ideally by a native speaker or language tool)
