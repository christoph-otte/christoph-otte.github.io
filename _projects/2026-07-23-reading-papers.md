# How to Read Scientific Papers

Reading research papers is a skill — and like any skill, it improves with deliberate practice. This page is for students who are new to engaging with primary literature.

---

## Why Read Papers?

Scientific papers are the primary record of scientific knowledge. Unlike textbooks, which synthesize and simplify, papers show you exactly what was done, what was found, and what was uncertain. Reading them teaches you:

- How scientific arguments are structured
- What current open questions in a field look like
- How to write your own paper (by example)

---

## You Do Not Have to Read Every Word

The most important skill in reading papers is knowing **what to read and in what order** for your purpose.

### Pass 1 — Title, Abstract, Figures (~5 minutes)

Decide if the paper is relevant to you. Read:
- The **title** and **abstract** carefully
- The **figures and their captions** (figures often tell the whole story)
- The **conclusion** (last paragraph of the Discussion or a dedicated Conclusion section)

After Pass 1, you should know: What did they do? What did they find? Is this relevant to my question?

### Pass 2 — Introduction and Discussion (~20–40 minutes)

If the paper is relevant, read:
- The **Introduction** to understand the motivation and context
- The **Discussion** to understand the interpretation of the results

You can still skip or skim the Methods and Results in detail for now.

### Pass 3 — Full paper, critically (~1–2 hours)

Only when you need to fully understand or use the work:
- Read the **Methods** carefully — could you reproduce this?
- Check the **Results** against the figures — do the numbers match the text?
- Look at the **supplementary material** if there is any
- Follow up on the key **references** you do not know

---

## How to Read Critically

Ask yourself these questions as you read:

**About the question:**
- What exactly is the research question?
- Is it clearly stated?

**About the methods:**
- Is the approach appropriate for the question?
- Are the controls sufficient?
- Could I reproduce this with the information given?

**About the results:**
- Are the figures clear? Are axes labeled with units?
- Are uncertainties and error bars reported?
- Does the data actually support the claims?

**About the interpretation:**
- Do the conclusions follow from the results?
- Are alternative explanations considered?
- Are the limitations honestly stated?

**About the literature:**
- Are the key references cited? Are there important papers missing?
- Is there a conflict of interest worth noting (funding sources, affiliations)?

## Taking Notes Effectively

Do not just highlight — annotate. Write in the margins (or in a note-taking tool):
- *"Main claim: …"*
- *"Unclear: why was method X chosen?"*
- *"Contradiction with [other paper]"*
- *"Interesting — follow up"*

A useful structure for a paper summary note:

```
Paper: [Author et al., Year, Journal]
DOI: ...

Question:
Methods (briefly):
Main finding:
Limitations:
Relevance to my work:
Questions I still have:
```

## Useful Tools

- **Connected Papers** — visualize citation networks around a paper
- **Semantic Scholar** — find related work and open-access versions
- **Unpaywall** (browser extension) — find legal free versions of paywalled papers
- **Zotero** — free reference manager for organizing and annotating papers
- **arXiv / bioRxiv / chemRxiv** — preprint servers with free access to recent work

---

## A Note on Preprints

Many papers first appear as preprints (on arXiv, bioRxiv, etc.) before peer review. These are not yet peer-reviewed and should be treated with extra scrutiny — but they are valuable for staying current. Always check if a peer-reviewed version exists before citing.

---

## Reading Papers to Write Your Own

If you are writing an article, read papers in your topic area with the following lens:

- How is the Introduction structured? How do they establish the gap?
- How specific are the Methods? What level of detail is expected?
- How do figures communicate the story?
- How do authors hedge their claims?
- What citation style is used?

Imitation is one of the best ways to learn scientific writing.
