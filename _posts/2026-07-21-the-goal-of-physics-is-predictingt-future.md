To achieve this, theories are formulated, models are developed and hypotheses are stated which are investigated in experiments. In physical science, a theory or a model can never be proved but only confirmed. In other words, one starts with the assumption that the theory at hand correctly describes Nature and then looks for evidence refuting this assumption: if the deviation of a measurement from the hypothesis is larger than the measuring inaccuracy, either the model or even the theory is disproved. Prominent examples are the discovery of Neptune changing the model of our solar system and Mercury's perihelion shift pointing towards a new theory of gravity.

In most cases, the usual workflow goes like this: A physical theory first invents completely arbitrarily physical quantities and specifies equations that relate them. A physical quantity is a variable that is linked to a measurement prescription, i.e. a rule how this quantity is to be measured in an experiment. Every physical equation is feedback from Nature that something has gone wrong in the conception of the quantities in the equations. For example, Einstein's famous equation $$E=mc^2$$, which has been confirmed in numerous experiments, is basically a feedback from nature that the two concepts behind mass and energy are actually the same.
